[![Indoor unit + outdoor unit](/assets/images/Erfahrungen-Matthias-WP.png)](/assets/images/Erfahrungen-Matthias-WP.png)

Since March 2024, we have had the Bosch Compress CS6800i AW 12 MB with the AW 4 OR-S outdoor unit, and so far we are very satisfied.
The outdoor unit is extremely quiet, the house and the domestic hot water reach the desired temperature, and the price was absolutely fair.
Competent heating specialists are located nearby and respond promptly when something is not working.
Bosch is also reachable and has already carried out two software updates to resolve faults.
We can therefore recommend the system with a clear conscience.

## Building

- Semi-detached house
- Year of construction: 1998
- Heated area: approx. 110 m<sup>2</sup>
- Type: underfloor heating + domestic hot water production
- Daily domestic hot water consumption: approx. 150l
- Heating limit temperature: 15 °C
- Standard outdoor temperature (NAT): -13.6 °C
- Heating load: 4.7 kW
- Previous gas consumption: 13,000-15,500 kWh/year

[![Building characteristic curve, min/max heating output and density function of the outdoor temperature](/assets/images/Erfahrungen-Matthias-HeizlastGebäudekennlinieATDichte.png)](/assets/images/Erfahrungen-Matthias-HeizlastGebäudekennlinieATDichte.png)

## Heat pump

### Heating system

- Indoor unit: Bosch Compress CS6800i AW 12 MB
  - Software Version: 12.11.1 (previously 9.6.1 and 23.08.08-5.35)
- Outdoor unit: AW 4 OR-S
  - Software Version: 9.15.0 (previously 9.6.0 and 23.08.08-5.35)
- Domestic hot water tank: WH 290 Stora domestic hot water tank (277 l)

### Design parameters

- Bivalence point: -8 °C<br/>
  At temperatures below -8 °C, the electric backup heater provides support.
  According to the [Bosch sizing tool](https://bosch-de-heatpump.thernovo.com/home), the electric backup heater requires only 100 kWh per year (< 1%).
- Cycling limit: 8 °C<br/>
  From 8 °C, the heat pump can no longer modulate to a lower output and must therefore cycle up to the heating limit temperature of 15 °C.
- Peak output coverage at the NAT: 66%<br/>
  At -14 °C, 66% of the heat (approx. 2.9 kW out of 4.3 kWh) is generated by the heat pump and 33% by the backup heater [[Bosch sizing tool](https://bosch-de-heatpump.thernovo.com/home)].

### Settings

- Temperature difference of the primary circuit (TC3-TC0): 3.5 K
- Differential pressure PC1: 150 mbar (during sustained cold temperatures, we increase it to 300 mbar)
- Flow temperature (NAT): 39 °C
- Heating limit temperature: 15 °C

In each of the first two years, our heat pump consumed approximately 3,100 kWh.
This produced 10,000 kWh of heat per year.
This is interesting, as it is significantly less than what our old, presumably very inefficient gas heating system required.
Presumably, one-third of the gas heating system's heat was lost through the chimney.

In the first two years, this resulted in a seasonal performance factor (SPF) of 3.2 and 3.3, respectively.
One reason why the seasonal performance factor is relatively low could be the fact that we have quite a lot of south-facing windows and, as soon as the sun shines and it gets warmer and the heat pump would deliver good efficiency values, our house is heated so much by the sun that the heat pump is no longer needed.
In addition, the standby consumption weighs relatively heavily for our very small heat pump.
This effect is significantly lower in larger systems with higher output.

Furthermore, compared with many other manufacturers, the Bosch/Buderus heat pump subtracts the energy 'lost' through defrosting from the generated energy.
The diagram below shows that the thermal output is negative, which even causes the performance factor to become negative.

Finally, it should be noted that the electrical energy, and therefore also the performance factor, includes the complete peripheral equipment.
This includes 2 pumps, the control system of the indoor and outdoor units, as well as the compressor and condensate pan heaters.
These consumers were not taken into account in the gas heating system's consumption and would actually have to be added on top.

The diagram shows the electrical and thermal output on an exemplary humid winter day with an outdoor temperature of 4 °C.

### Domestic hot water

We have optimized our settings so that [daily domestic hot water production](/en/docs/optimierungen/#warmwasserbereitung) almost always takes place between 13:00 and 15:00.
For this, approximately 1-2 kWh of electrical energy is used—with a performance factor of 2-4, depending on the outdoor temperature.

The temperature loss of our domestic hot water tank over 24 hours is:

- 4.3 K with circulation (2x5 minutes)
- 3 K without circulation

[![Domestic hot water temperature loss](/assets/images/Erfahrungen-Matthias-WWTemperaturverlust.png)](/assets/images/Erfahrungen-Matthias-WWTemperaturverlust.png)

### Sizing

Over 365 days, the heat pump ran for 4,000 hours, and the compressor had to start approximately 630 times during this period.
On average, this resulted in 1.7 starts/day, and a compressor run lasted 6.3 hours.
In summer, without heating operation, there was one start every day for domestic hot water production, while in winter, after domestic hot water production, the system was usually still heated up enough that the compressor was allowed to take a break for some time.
One compressor start per day was therefore almost guaranteed throughout the entire year.
The remaining approximately 300 compressor starts were due to cycling during the transitional period.
In my view, these are quite good values, and the heat pump seems to be well sized at the cycling limit (approx. 8 °C).

This naturally raises the question of whether the good sizing at the cycling limit had negative effects on the bivalence point and whether the backup heater had to be switched on very often.
This is not the case.
In the first year, the backup heater ran exactly once for approximately 30 minutes, and in the second year, on the rather cold days at -12 °C, for a total of approximately 3 hours.

### Sustainability

Recently, I wondered how much of the energy used for our heat pump actually comes from renewable sources.
To find out, I created a panel in [Grafana](https://bosch-buderus-wp.github.io/en/docs/smarthome/grafana) that breaks down the energy consumption of our heat pump on a daily basis as follows:

- HP energy originating from the PV system (100% renewable)
- HP grid consumption:
  - Renewable share of the German electricity mix on that day
  - Fossil share of the German electricity mix on that day

This allows me to determine the actual share of renewable energy in the electricity consumption of our heat pump.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><i class="fa-solid fa-calculator"></i>Calculation formula</summary>
<br>
\\[
EEAnteil = \frac{E_{PV} \; + \; E_{Netz} \cdot EEAnteil_{Netz}}{E_{gesamt}}
\\]
\\[
= \frac{E_{gesamt} \cdot Autarkie \; + \; E_{gesamt} \cdot (1 - Autarkie) \cdot EEAnteil_{Netz}}{E_{gesamt}} \\
\\]
\\[
= Autarkie \; + \; (1 - Autarkie) \cdot EEAnteil_{Netz}
\\]

The data on the German electricity mix comes from [Greengrid Compass](https://www.greengrid-compass.eu/).

</details>

The daily breakdown into PV energy, renewable share, fossil share, and the resulting share of renewable energy in the 2nd year of operation is shown in the following diagram:

[![Actual share of renewable energy](https://i.ibb.co/rKhvgn1G/Matthias-Realer-Anteil-Erneuerbarer-Enerigen.png)](https://i.ibb.co/rKhvgn1G/Matthias-Realer-Anteil-Erneuerbarer-Enerigen.png)

On an annual average, the share of renewable energy was an astonishing 85%.
The share was naturally somewhat lower in winter, but still usually above 50%.
And with every additional wind turbine and every additional solar system, the share continues to increase 💚

## Connection insulation

Since there was no insulation for the outdoor unit connections when we purchased our heat pump, I made the insulation myself from a leftover piece of screen-printing board, rock wool, waterproof wood glue and a few stainless-steel screws.
